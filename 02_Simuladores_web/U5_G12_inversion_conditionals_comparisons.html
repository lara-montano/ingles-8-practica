💡 How it works

Delete if, front the auxiliary

Formal English can delete if and invert — with exactly three verbs: had, should and were. The auxiliary jumps in front of the subject and the comma stays. Pick a pattern and watch the swap. (Se borra if y el auxiliar salta delante del sujeto; solo had, should, were.)

    ⚖️ After than and as

    Inversion in comparisons — optional elegance

    After than or as, formal prose may invert subject and auxiliary when the subject is a full noun phrase. It is optional — but the exam lists it among the correct answers, so read it as right, not strange. (Opcional, pero correcta: reconócela en el examen.)

    🛑 Error clinic

    The seven traps Spanish speakers fall into

    • ✘ Had she would have known the cost… → ✔ Had she known the cost, she would have canceled it. The condition never carries would. Only the main clause does. (El would va en la principal, nunca en la condición.)
    • ✘ If had she known… → ✔ Had she known… / If she had known… One or the other: keep if or invert, never both.
    • ✘ Hadn’t she known… → ✔ Had she not known… Negatives do not contract in an if-less conditional: Had it not been for the backup, we would have lost everything.
    • ✘ Should he will need assistance… → ✔ Should he need assistance, the desk is staffed around the clock. Should + subject + base verb. No future form inside the condition.
    • ✘ Knew I the answer, I would tell you. → ✔ If I knew the answer, I would tell you. Only had, were, should can invert. A lexical verb keeps if.
    • ✘ Had we known, had we acted. → ✔ Had we known, we would have acted. After the comma the main clause keeps normal order.
    • ✘ …more faults than did he. → ✔ …more faults than he did. With a pronoun subject there is no inversion after than or as.
