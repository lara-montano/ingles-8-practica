💡 How it works

Front the trigger, and the sentence swaps slots

English word order is rigid, so breaking it on purpose is a signal. Push a negative or restrictive expression — or a place expression — into slot 1, and the machinery behind it moves. Read the column numbers, not the words. (Adelanta el disparador y observa qué pieza se mueve.)

    🚪 The doorway: echo inversions

    So do I · Neither did we · Nor does the report

    These tiny echoes run on the identical mechanism as Never have we…: negative-or-additive word first, then the auxiliary, then the subject. Master the small version and the solemn one comes free. (Misma máquina, versión de bolsillo.)

    🚫 When NOT to invert

    Six places where the machinery stays off

    The trigger is not at the front✔ The simulation rarely matches reality.
    Inversion is paid for by fronting. No fronting, no swap.
    Only + subject✔ Only the director knows.
    Nothing moves. Inversion needs only + adverbial (time, place, condition, means): ✔ Only then did we understand.
    Place inversion with an object✘ On the table put the technician the tools.
    ✔ On the table, the technician put the tools. The verb must be intransitive (position or motion).
    Place inversion with a pronoun✔ Here comes the bus. (noun subject)
    ✔ Here it comes. (pronoun subject — no inversion)
    Not only inside the clause✔ It not only failed, but it failed publicly.
    Only the fronted version inverts: Not only did it fail…
    Not until — the wrong clause✘ Not until the audit did end, we learned the truth.
    ✔ Not until the audit ended did we learn the truth. The main clause inverts.
    🏛️ Why English works this way

    Inversion is a V2 fossil

    A thousand years ago English, like modern German, kept its verb in second position no matter what came first. Modern English dropped that rule — except in fossils: questions, here comes / there goes, and the fronted negatives of this unit. Push never or rarely into the emphasis seat and the old grammar wakes up and demands the verb right behind it. That is why inversion sounds solemn and slightly ancient: it is ancient. Formal English keeps the ruin standing precisely because its rarity is what gives it weight. (No es un truco: es gramática antigua que sigue operando.)

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    What Spanish speakers get wrong here

    • ✘ Never I have seen such results. · ✘ Never saw we such results. → ✔ Never have I seen such results. Do not invert the main verb and do not leave the subject first: the auxiliary goes before the subject.
    • ✘ Rarely the model fails. → ✔ Rarely does the model fail. A fronted negative requires inversion; it is not optional emphasis.
    • ✘ Rarely does she goes out. → ✔ Rarely does she go out. After borrowed do/does/did the main verb returns to its base form.
    • ✘ Here it comes the bus. → ✔ Here comes the bus. · ✔ Here it comes. One subject only: a noun inverts, a pronoun does not.
    • ✘ On the table put the technician the tools. → ✔ On the table, the technician put the tools. Place inversion is blocked when the verb has an object.
    • ✘ Never we don’t inspect the seals. → ✔ Never do we inspect the seals. One negation per clause: the trigger already carries it.
